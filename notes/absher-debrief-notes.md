# Debrief with Prof. Absher — September 15, 2026

Debrief on the September 11 Polsinelli call. On the call: Prof. Evan Absher, Anthony, Camron,
Kirby, Mandy. About 38 minutes, transcribed.

Absher said several times he was "thinking out loud." Ideas below are recorded as what was
said, not as decisions.

---

## How it opened

Anthony summarized the Sean call: Sean framed the barrier as awareness rather than cost;
advisors, especially investment bankers paid on a fee, don't recommend ESOPs; seller financing
documents can be standardized but senior lenders insist on their own forms; plan documents are
available pre-approved. Anthony said he wasn't sure where the project should go from there.

Absher said Sean had told him the same thing when they met the previous spring, and asked the
team why they thought it was an awareness issue rather than a transaction-cost issue.

## Absher: a delivery problem, not an awareness problem

> "less that it's an awareness issue and it's more of a delivery issue. We have a delivery
> problem, right?"

> "if you think about it for two seconds, it's not awareness, it's incentives of the people of
> the current actors."

His reasoning: even if owners are told about ESOPs earlier, "they're going to take that
material, that awareness that they've gained, right back to their advisors who they trust," and
get the same response, because those advisors still aren't incentivized or familiar.

## Absher: the facility idea

> "you create a facility that's incentivized to originate these deals."

Pull in businesses and financing first; technical advisors follow because the deals are getting
done that way — "you either help and get a piece of it or you don't and you're cut out
essentially. Because I think that's the only way you can break through that technical advisor
push or resistance."

## Absher: Novel Capital as a model

He put [Novel Capital](https://novelcapital.com/) in the chat — a Kansas City revenue-based
financing fund started by people he has worked with, mostly from the Kauffman Foundation.
Revenue-based financing is a structured-return investment: more expensive than debt, but without
the uncapped upside of equity.

What he pointed to: "they've sort of automated and standardized as much of the underwriting as
they possibly can so that they can look at a lot of different deals, underwrite deals that they
might not otherwise, that might have been somewhat marginal." Their site has narrow intake
questions and companies "self-select. So it's like lead generation."

## UPL and the form-recommendation idea

Mandy raised that part of what the team had imagined AI doing would be counseling, which it
can't do.

Anthony described an idea: a business owner inputs the three big decisions Sean named (1042 or
not, and the other two) and the tool produces a suggested form. Anthony said recommending one
form over another likely crosses the UPL line.

Absher: "More than likely. More than likely. I think that's a really good idea. I don't think
it's a problem that a lot of this stuff has already been done. I think that's a good thing for
this project."

## Due diligence prep list

Anthony noted Sean's point that an ESOP is still an M&A deal, with diligence the trustee's
fiduciary duty requires.

Absher suggested the world "is probably not ready for automated due diligence, especially the
legal world," and floated a due diligence **prep** list instead — part awareness ("did you know
that you're actually in a really good place for this thing?") and part readiness: "it's again,
not recommending anything. It's just saying like, these are areas that you could look into, or
these are the types of things you need to have."

## Running out of hours

Mandy asked whether there would be enough work to fill 135 hours each.

Absher: "just because the list has shrunk like a number of items doesn't mean that those
individual items don't have a ton of work." And: "just something simple that works is like
hugely value additive… it's an improvement over the existing status quo and that's always a big
deal."

## Not having a project yet

Anthony said it had been hard to start without a defined project, and that each direction they
dug into turned out to be a dead end. Kirby agreed.

Absher: finding the right question "is almost always the biggest part or the most difficult
part of any sort of like creation," and dead ends are "very normal and very expected. Not a bad
thing. And it's not a waste of time."

## Camron: not enough ESOP attorneys

Camron raised Sean's estimate of 50 to 100 ESOP attorneys in the country: "it could literally
just be an issue if there's not enough attorneys in the area."

Absher tied it to the advisor problem — a non-ESOP lawyer says "I'm not an ESOP attorney. I
don't know this deal… And I'm not going to hand you over to Sean because you're my client."
He floated "a CLE educational piece" — "We've created this tool for practitioners." He noted
Sean has "probably got more work than he knows what to do with."

## Absher: transaction cost is still in play

> "When a lawyer says it's not the transaction costs, he's excluding from his mind his own
> fees."

Polsinelli rates are part of the transaction cost; on a $5 million deal, $200,000–$250,000 of
legal fees is a sizable share.

> "can we reduce the complexity of the cost for advisors that are not currently ESOP providers in
> order to open up access to lawyers and transactions to companies that could not afford
> [Polsinelli]?"

He also framed cost more broadly: "If we reduce the cost of customer acquisition, that's
reducing transaction costs."

## Absher: two-sided market

As he laid it out:

- **Demand side:** businesses — "are you a good ESOP?"
- **Supply side:** lawyers and technical assistance providers who could help but "don't know
  enough about ESOPs, don't feel comfortable doing it." The tool would take the documents that
  are already standard, and what someone like Sean knows, and make them "legible for a
  non-expert that's still a lawyer or technical provider."
- **Pull mechanism:** "if theoretically we had a fund that said anything that comes out of this
  tool will be qualified for investment from this regional facility we're putting together. And
  then that would be the pull mechanism that would pull everybody into it."

## Deal size range

Camron relayed Sean's numbers: smallest deal about $5.5 million, around $20 million to make sense
for Polsinelli, and an EOT for companies too small.

Anthony, from podcasts and Sean: a company needs to be profitable, needs a CFO and an HR function,
and needs a meaningful level of EBITDA (the figure is unclear in the transcript — roughly $800,000
to $1 million).

Absher confirmed Sean was describing deal size, and walked the math: at a 5x multiple, $1 million
of EBITDA is a $5 million deal, so a $5 million floor and a ~$1 million EBITDA floor are
consistent. (EBITDA is earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization.)

> "I wonder if we can play in the 5 to 20 range."

That would be roughly $1–5 million of EBITDA, or "maybe like 2 to 8."

Anthony added from his notes that Sean did a $5.5 million deal that felt too small, is pitching a
$14 million one that is small, and that Polsinelli mostly does $40–60 million and up — so the
range may run higher than Absher's figure.

Absher asked the team to think about where that limit and range actually are.

Camron noted that aiming below Polsinelli's clients might make Sean more willing to help.

## Absher: who the tool is for

Absher's view was that owners able to hire a firm like Polsinelli would go there directly and
wouldn't use the tool, though Sean or his associates might.

> "our ideal customer is a lawyer or business that doesn't know Sean, wouldn't pay for Sean…
> has a lawyer… and then we can provide the tool to help the lawyer do this in a way that they
> feel secure and safe and they can recommend this to their client."

## Hand-off

He suggested the tool could be handed to the Missouri Center for Employee Ownership: "we created
this tool, you should go around and just… all your businesses run through this."

## Suggested next steps

- Start building toward the question: "are you a good candidate for this? What do we need to
  answer that question?"
- Once there is something to show, go back to Sean with it and with questions — such as "where
  are common areas that businesses need to do a lot of work to get prepared for."
- Think through where the deal-size limit and range really are.
