# What makes a company a good ESOP candidate — research memo

Research findings, September 15, 2026. **Not independently verified by the team.** Every figure
below is a speaker's or source's claim, not a fact, and none of it has a `SOURCES.md` entry. Read
the sources before relying on any of it. Nothing here is site copy.

This memo is built from eleven research notes that are not in the project:

- **Seven on podcast episodes:** company fit, sound transactions, after the sale, small companies
  and alternatives, seller readiness and advisors, Menke's ESOP Radio series, and 21 Hats and
  How2Exit.
- **Four on written sources:** existing fit tools, the research literature, regulators and courts,
  and the Kansas City ecosystem and financing.

Those notes hold the episode links, timestamps, and page cites. Ask Anthony for a specific cite.

- **Incentives.** Almost every podcast voice earns fees from ESOP deals, and almost every research
  source is published or funded by a group that promotes employee ownership. Both are noted where
  it matters.
- **Legal authorities** are leads. The underlying notes mark each one by how much of it was
  actually read.
- **How2Exit E88** was read from YouTube's auto-generated transcript.

## The short answer

Across the sources, a good ESOP has four things going for it:

1. **Cash flow that can carry the debt.** Steady, predictable profit. This is the one requirement
   every source shares. An ESOP does not fix a weak business ("Good business, good ESOP. Bad
   business, bad ESOP." — Aspengren).
2. **A business that runs without the owner.** A successor team, or at least a credible path to
   one. Owner dependence is the most-cited non-financial red flag.
3. **An owner who wants what an ESOP actually delivers.** Fair market value, not a strategic
   premium; a large share paid over years through seller notes; independence and legacy. Sources
   describe an owner who is not really leaving, or who wants top dollar, as a poor fit.
4. **A sound price, reached through a process that would survive scrutiny.** Courts judge the
   trustee's process: an independent, documented investigation that tests management's projections
   instead of deferring to the appraiser. Advisors name overvaluation as the main way ESOPs go
   wrong, and owners name over-leverage as their main regret. A seller who knows the price is too
   high can be personally liable.

Size matters mainly because fixed costs and payroll-based contribution limits bite hardest at the
bottom. After the sale, the biggest threat to employee ownership lasting is **the company being
sold**, not failing.

**No study validates the business criteria.** No research links pre-deal company traits to whether
an ESOP closed or lasted. Every size threshold is a practitioner rule of thumb, and the rules
conflict. The legal requirements (below) are the only hard lines.

## What the intake would have to cover

Ranked by how consistently sources support each factor.

| Factor | What sources say | Support |
|---|---|---|
| **Cash flow and profitability** | Stable enough to service acquisition debt and later share buybacks. Erratic earnings are disqualifying. | Universal |
| **Owner dependence and successors** | Business runs without the owner. Finnell calls a successor team "imperative." Doeren Mayhew and Lazear accept a credible path to building one. | Universal; strictness varies |
| **Owner goals** | Price vs. legacy; cash at closing vs. total proceeds; staying vs. leaving; partial vs. full sale. Asked by 9 of 10 readable existing tools. | Near universal |
| **Entity type and ownership** | § 1042 deferral requires shares of a domestic C corporation, a seller who has held them 3+ years, and an ESOP owning 30%+ after the sale. S corporation ESOPs must pass § 409(p). Stock options, SARs, phantom stock, and deferred compensation count as synthetic equity in that test. | Statute and regulation text |
| **Size** | Conflicting floors in EBITDA, revenue, and headcount. See next section. | Contested |
| **Financial reporting** | Reviewed or audited statements and a strong controller or CFO (Menke). DOL's process agreements tell trustees to request five years of audited, unqualified statements. | Strong; settlement terms plus practitioners |
| **Projections** | Must hold up against about five years of history and peer companies. Untested management projections are a recurring basis for liability (*Brundle*, *Donovan*). | Merits judgments plus practitioners |
| **Customer concentration** | One or two customers dominating revenue is a red flag. In *Brundle*, about 70% of revenue from two contracts was valued as if low-risk. | Repeated; one merits judgment |
| **Structure** | Multiple legal entities are hard to appraise or put under one ESOP (Goltz, Weinzweig). VC- or PE-backed companies are generally not candidates (Menke). | A few sources |
| **Workforce** | Headcount floors (below). A workforce retiring all at once strains repurchase. Departing employees can require the company to buy back their shares (§ 409(h)). | Repeated; statute for repurchase |
| **Culture** | Companies that already give employees information and a voice do best (Bonham, Aspengren). Open-book as a prerequisite is argued mainly by people who sell open-book training. | Repeated, with an incentive caveat |
| **HR function** | **No source named it as a requirement.** | Not found |

**Other disqualifiers sources named:**
- An owner staying on with family succession in mind (Goltz).
- A lifestyle business where the owner "owns their job" (Menke).
- Exposure to a dominant competitor or a regulatory change that could wipe the company out (Lape).
- Pending litigation, or a government investigation into the company's accounting (Finnell;
  *Brundle*).

## What a trustee will ask for (material for a prep list)

Prof. Absher floated a due diligence *prep* list, framed as "these are the types of things you need
to have." The sources converge on what the buyer's side will examine. Stated here as what trustees
and regulators look for, not as advice to any company.

| Area | What gets examined | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **Financial statements** | Five years, audited and unqualified. If they're missing, the trustee must document why relying on them is still prudent. | GreatBanc process agreement § 5 |
| **Projections** | Who prepared them and whether that person is conflicted, e.g., bonuses tied to the price. Tested against five-year history and comparable companies. Support documented for anything above history. | GreatBanc § 4; *Brundle* |
| **Prior valuations and sale attempts** | Earlier appraisals, prior offers, and failed sales. In *Brundle* the trustee never asked about a much lower valuation from months earlier. | Horizon Bank and First Bankers agreements § 6; *Brundle* |
| **Risk disclosure** | Concentration, key customers, anything the appraiser needs to know. In *Bruister* the appraiser wasn't told about the sole client's policy changes. | *Bruister*; Horizon § 4 |
| **Control** | What the ESOP actually gets for a control price: board seats and voting. No control premium if the sellers keep control. | *Brundle*; Horizon and First Bankers § 11 |
| **Debt capacity** | Whether the company can service the debt under stress. Acquisition debt principal may not exceed the stock's fair market value. | GreatBanc §§ 4(i), 10 |
| **Existing equity-like arrangements** | SARs, phantom stock, deferred compensation. They count in § 409(p) testing and must be deducted from value. | Treas. Reg. § 1.409(p)-1(f); *Brundle* Part II.C |
| **Timeline** | Enough review time. A seller's tax deadline driving a two-month deal was held against the trustee. | *Brundle* Part II.B; Horizon § 6 |
| **Operations** | Normalized adjusted EBITDA, working capital discipline, documented processes, no "hero" employees. | Doeren Mayhew episodes |

**Caution.** The process agreements are settlements binding the trustees that signed them, not
regulations. They are the most detailed public statement of what DOL expects.

## Where the size floor actually sits

**Nobody frames the floor in equity value.** The ~$20M figure from the Polsinelli call reflects
Polsinelli's own deal flow. Everyone else uses EBITDA, revenue, or headcount:

| Source (what they sell) | Floor |
|---|---|
| Phil Hayes, 21 Hats (small-deal ESOP consultant, no success fee) | ~$800K–$1M net income or EBITDA; comfortable above ~20 employees for an S corp |
| Kelly Finnell, 2025 interviews (ESOP consultant) | $1M adjusted EBITDA minimum, $2M better; at least 35 employees |
| Chris Michael (sells EOT deals) | ESOP gets "nervous" at ~20 employees and $1M EBITDA |
| Keith Davenport, then MOCEO director | ~20 employees, ~$1.5M EBITDA, or ~$10M revenue |
| Menke podcast (ESOP investment bank) | "A couple million of EBITDA," "case by case" |
| Menke website (same firm) | ~$200K pretax income; 7 employees (C corp) or 12 (S corp); $5M+ revenue |
| Larry Kaplan, How2Exit E88 (CSG Partners, ESOP investment bank) | CSG works at $2.5M+ EBITDA; says other firms do ESOPs for companies earning $500–700K a year |
| RBG Capital (ESOP advisory) | ~$1M EBITDA; ~20 employees |
| NCEO toolkit | 15–20 employees |
| Project Equity (ESOP article) | 40+ employees; ~$750K EBITDA |
| Victor Aspengren (consultant, 2021) | ~$5M sales and 25 employees |
| Salvatore Tirabassi (fractional CFO) | $5M+ net income — the outlier |

What follows from this:

- **The $800K–$1M figure raised in the Absher debrief traces to Phil Hayes on 21 Hats.** He said net
  income "or if we translate that into EBITDA, cash flow." He did not attach a CFO or HR requirement.
- **Headcount may be a better screen than deal value.** The $5.5M / 18-employee deal described on
  the Polsinelli call falls below most headcount floors.
  - One reason given is that contribution limits tie what the ESOP can buy to payroll (Aspengren;
    Rosen). That limit still needs a primary source.
  - Another is S corp § 409(p) testing (Hayes). The statute says a plan year fails when
    "disqualified persons" own at least 50% of shares. A disqualified person is anyone with 10%
    individually, or 20% with family.
  - With few employees, each account is a bigger share of the total, so that test is easier to
    fail. **That last step is an inference from the text, not something a source states.**
- **Prof. Absher's "5 to 20" range sits inside the market, at its lower edge.**
  - Menke's own podcast examples fall there: a ~$3M-EBITDA consulting firm at 5–6.5x, and a $15M
    deal done entirely on a seller note.
  - Finnell says deals now more commonly run $10M–$100M.
  - The lower half of the range is where headcount floors, fixed costs, and lender minimums all
    start to bind.
- **Even one firm's numbers don't agree with themselves.** Menke's podcast, candidate-profile page,
  and website give three different floors. These read best as ranges from named sources, not as a
  threshold.

## Cost: both earlier views are partly right

- **The Polsinelli view holds at large deal sizes.** On the call, cost was described as no higher
  than a third-party sale. NCEO's Corey Rosen puts ESOP transaction costs at 2–4% of deal value
  against 5–9% for other sales. NCEO's FAQ says 4–9%. Tenor's Schnabl also called an ESOP sale
  cheaper.
- **Prof. Absher's view holds at the bottom of the range.**
  - Rosen: "The smaller the company, the bigger the percentage."
  - Setup-cost figures span a wide range: ~$40K (Kalb's own partial ESOP), $125K minimum (NCEO
    toolkit), $150K–$400K (Rosen), and $200K–$500K (NCEO FAQ). No cost survey backs any of them.
  - Annual carry: $20K–$30K (NCEO, RBG). A trustee alone runs ~$10K a year (Kalb) or ~$20K (the
    Polsinelli call). Larry Kaplan (CSG) put annual valuation at $15K–$35K, administration at ~$6–7K
    for under 20 employees, and a bare-bones ongoing cost at ~$30K.
  - Illustrative arithmetic: a $300K setup is 6% of a $5M deal and 1.5% of a $20M deal.
  - Scharf's sellers named **ongoing annual cost** as a main reason they walked away. The smallest
    firms felt compliance complexity most.
- **The cost of doing it cheaply.**
  - The cases show what a thin process costs later. In *Brundle*, damages were $29.8M, itemized by
    each valuation error.
  - *Su v. Bowers* is the counterpoint. The trustee billed 30 hours and the deal closed 11 days
    after the trustee was appointed. DOL still lost at trial, largely on flaws in its own expert's
    valuation.
  - The "B team" concern from the Polsinelli call has support here. Nothing measures how often it
    happens.
- **Where the brief's "$80,000" comes from:** Rosen's "Twelve Bogus Reasons Not to Do an ESOP" says
  even very small companies face "$80,000 or more." The brief's figure appears to trace there.

## Awareness vs. delivery

**The sources lean toward Prof. Absher's framing for the owners a tool would actually reach.**

- **Awareness side.** Rosen, Menke, and Bernstein's 2020 survey of 250 non-ESOP firms (ESCA
  commissioned it) call awareness the main barrier. That survey measured owners who never looked.
- **Delivery side.** Scharf (Rutgers, 2024) interviewed eight sellers who seriously considered an
  ESOP and stopped. They walked away over:
  - cash at closing (more than half)
  - cost, especially annual cost
  - complexity
  - a thin leadership bench
  - advisors steering them elsewhere
- **The awareness camp's own stories support delivery.**
  - Rosen tells owners to educate themselves *before* hiring advisors, because advisors "develop
    favorite approaches."
  - Jay Goltz studied ESOPs for four years. His accountant of 25 years and his lawyer waved him
    off. He still hasn't chosen a path.
  - Larry Kaplan (CSG, a sell-side ESOP bank) opens his takeaways by saying owners' professional
    advisors "have very limited information" about ESOPs. He names the belief that owners must
    personally guarantee the ESOP debt as the biggest misconception.
- **"CPAs are less conflicted" is contested.**
  - The Georgia center, an ESOP advisor (Middendorp), and an Australian CPA (West) all say CPAs
    often don't understand ESOPs and route owners to M&A intermediaries.
  - The Georgia center still treats CPAs as the channel worth training.
  - CSG Partners is actively marketing ESOPs through accounting-profession podcasts.
- **Success fees aren't limited to generalist bankers.** Hayes, Middendorp, and Menke's own series
  warn about percentage fees *inside* the ESOP industry.

The two views describe different populations. Awareness gets an owner to the door. Advisors, cash
at closing, and cost stop the ones who walk through it.

## Cash at closing is where deals die, and the local financing gap is real

- **Cash at closing.** The ~30% figure from the Polsinelli call is in line with other sources:
  - 25–40% bank-funded (Menke, 2026 examples)
  - 30–50% (Schnabl, 2019)
  - ~40% (Finnell)
  - 20–40% (Middendorp)
- **Seller note terms.** The 7–10 years from the Polsinelli call is at the long end. Others: 4–7
  (Lape), 5–7 (Schnabl, Finnell), 5–10 (Middendorp), with a ~10-year warrant horizon (Doeren
  Mayhew). Rosen put current seller-note rates at ~9–11%.
- **Seller financing carries its own legal flag.** In *Allen v. GreatBanc*, a pleading-stage ruling
  and not a merits finding, the court treated seller financing at a high rate as one reason an
  overpayment claim was plausible. It read that as "indicating that outside funding was not
  available." By statute, an ESOP loan must carry a rate no higher than "reasonable."
- **No local lender serves a $5–20M employee ownership deal.**
  - AltCap, the local CDFI, tops out at $500K.
  - MOCEO's "Capital Raising/Lending" directory category is empty.
  - National options have floors: Apis & Heritage lists $4–10M EBITDA and 40+ employees; Northwest
    Bank lists $2M+ EBITDA; JPMorgan (a KC task force partner) and Enterprise Bank publish no size.
- **Federal programs are constrained.**
  - NCEO reported in March 2026 that an SBA citizenship notice counts ESOP participants as indirect
    owners, and that some banks stopped making SBA ESOP loans as a result.
  - Treasury allows SSBCI funds for employee ownership loans under $5M. No Missouri or Kansas
    program uses them for that.
- **A model.** New Jersey's law, signed September 4, 2026, reimburses up to 90% of a feasibility
  study (capped at $35K) for companies with 20+ employees. It also creates a state revolving loan
  fund for employee ownership transitions.

This supports the brief's hypothesis that the gap is financing that understands the transaction,
and Prof. Absher's facility idea. It is a map of what exists, not a test of whether a fund would
work.

## After the sale: what makes an ESOP last

- **Education takes years.** Brian Allen (Precision Walls) estimated about four years before
  employees believed in it. His top regret was not investing more in education. Cathedral Holdings
  wished it had started open-book management at closing instead of four years later.
- **Over-leverage is the regret owners name.** Cathedral failed its bank covenants within two
  quarters at 4x senior leverage. The sellers put money back in, and said they'd use more seller
  financing next time.
- **Repurchase is planned for from the start.**
  - Departing participants can require the company to buy back non-traded shares "under a fair
    valuation formula" (§ 409(h)).
  - Cathedral keeps three years of repurchase obligations in cash.
  - Joe Marx (Principal) notes that redeeming shares favors the largest holders, while recycling
    spreads the benefit.
- **Being sold is the main threat.**
  - The ESOP Association's CEO calls it a "leaky bucket."
  - Older studies cited secondhand put 51% or 61% of ESOP terminations down to sales.
  - Speakers disagree on whether an owner can prevent it. Kalb says board control does. Hayes says
    the trustee must weigh a strong offer.
  - Cathedral adopted an unsolicited-offer policy and became a benefit corporation.
  - Larry Kaplan (CSG) tells the sale as a win. A 40-employee insurance agency sold 49% to an
    ESOP, then sold within about five years at close to double the trustee's ~9x value, and
    employees split ~$70M. He said he would have liked the ESOP to continue.
  - *Brundle* adds a legal wrinkle: a later sale to a competitor did not prove the original price
    was fair, and did not offset damages.
- **Size and failure.** In NCEO's 2007 interviews (30 companies, not representative), companies
  that ended their ESOP without a sale were smaller: median 80 employees and $17M revenue, against
  190 and $25M for long-running ESOPs.
- **Causation is unproven.** Research finds ESOP companies survive longer. Kruse notes that more
  stable firms may simply be the ones that adopt ESOPs.

## What already exists, and the gap

Twelve existing tools, checklists, and intake forms were surveyed.

- **Every firm-run intake takes contact information first** and delivers its read through an
  advisor: Menke, ESOP Builders, ESOP Partners, and CSG (contact only).
- **Every static checklist gives no read at all.** NCEO, RBG, Project Equity, and the Colorado
  center list questions; the owner grades themselves.
- **CSG Partners has no tool.** Its "ESOP Quiz" link opened a general services page. Its only
  route is contacting the firm.
- **Menke's questionnaire fields couldn't be seen.** The form loads by JavaScript, and the result is
  an advisor call.
- **No existing tool:**
  - turns an owner's answers into a list of what to prepare
  - is built for the owner's lawyer or CPA
  - sources its thresholds
  - frames fit by deal value
  - is local
- **MOCEO has no intake.** Its forms collect contact details only.
- **Scharf recommends this kind of tool.** She calls for "an easy to use website" that walks owners
  through the first steps (pp. 8–9). The page still needs checking before it is quoted.

**One implication, not decided.** The size thresholds conflict and none is research-backed. That
fits the project rule that the intake reports factors and considerations, never a verdict: showing
the range of named practitioner rules is more accurate than a pass/fail line. The legal
requirements (§ 1042, § 409(p)) and the trustee checklist above are different in kind. They are
published rules and published settlement terms.

## Regulators and courts

- **No valuation regulation exists, and the project brief has its status wrong.**
  - The current Unified Agenda (RIN 1210-AC20) targets **November 2026 for a *proposed* rule**, not
    final regulations.
  - DOL's January 2025 proposal was **withdrawn before publication**. Its Federal Register public
    inspection copy is stamped "THIS DOCUMENT WAS WITHDRAWN."
  - A 1988 proposal was never finalized.
  - The target date has slipped from December 2024 to January 2026 to November 2026.
  - SECURE 2.0 § 346 directed DOL to issue this guidance (cited, not read).
- **DOL enforcement has eased, as podcast speakers said.**
  - ESOPs are not on EBSA's current list of national enforcement projects (DOL page, viewed
    Sept. 15, 2026).
  - The January 15, 2026 release reportedly removing them returned an error and was not read.
  - Field Assistance Bulletin 2026-01 (April 14, 2026) says pending ESOP valuation investigations
    must be reviewed against a "principle of fairness" until DOL issues valuation standards.
  - **Rosen's investigation and case counts have no primary source.** No ESOP-specific DOL
    statistics were found, so those figures are not usable.
- **The legal standard hasn't changed with enforcement priorities.**
  - The trustee must show "a prudent investigation in the circumstances then prevailing"
    (*Donovan v. Cunningham*, 716 F.2d at 1467–68; *Perez v. Bruister*, 823 F.3d at 262–63).
  - An appraisal "is not a magic wand" (*Donovan*, 716 F.2d at 1474).
  - *Allen v. GreatBanc* was not brought by DOL. *Cunningham v. Cornell* (2025, not an ESOP case)
    puts the burden of pleading ERISA § 408 exemptions on the defendants.
  - Whether that carries over to ESOP adequate-consideration defenses is an inference to confirm.
- **Warning signs best supported by merits judgments:**
  - the seller or seller's lawyer shaping the valuation (*Bruister*)
  - untested management projections (*Brundle*, *Donovan*)
  - a stale appraisal, or an appraiser not given key risks (*Donovan*, *Bruister*)
  - a control premium when the sellers kept control (*Brundle*)
  - a seller-deadline rush (*Brundle*)
  - advisors with repeat-business ties to the seller's banker (*Brundle*)
- **Sound process looks like *Keach v. U.S. Trust*.** The trustee hired its own advisor,
  interviewed management, visited facilities, called the forecasts too optimistic, and negotiated
  about 20% below the ask. It won even though the company later went bankrupt ("prudence, not
  prescience").
- **Sellers are exposed too.**
  - In *Walsh v. Vinoskey* (4th Cir. 2021), a selling owner was liable as a knowing participant
    because he knew the price exceeded fair market value. The evidence included a ~40% jump over
    the same appraiser's prior values with no change in performance.
  - In *Bruister*, the seller's family LLC was held jointly and severally liable.
  - The seller's own exposure is therefore part of what makes a good ESOP.
- **Red flags are not proof.** In *Su v. Bowers* (9th Cir. 2023), DOL lost at trial despite a fast
  deal and a 30-hour trustee engagement. That ruling came in a fee appeal, not a merits holding.
- **Missouri and Kansas courts** (Eighth and Tenth Circuits):
  - ***Martin v. Feilen*** (8th Cir. 1992).
    - **Standard:** conflicts are not per se unlawful for ESOP fiduciaries. A fiduciary with dual
      loyalties must make "a careful and impartial investigation," and courts look at whether the
      fiduciaries "relied on outside advisors."
    - **Liability:** it came from insiders manipulating discounts. The ESOP paid full value while
      insiders bought a month later at a 20% minority discount.
    - **Outside accountants** who structured the deals were held to be fiduciaries and barred from
      serving ERISA plans.
  - ***Herman v. Mercantile Bank*** (8th Cir. 1998, a Missouri company) cuts the other way.
    - A trustee who made no good-faith valuation effort escaped liability, because a hypothetical
      prudent fiduciary would have paid the same price.
    - Four judges would have reheard it en banc.
    - It sits uneasily with *Bruister*'s holding that a price landing in range by luck doesn't meet
      the burden. How later Eighth Circuit cases treat it is unchecked, so the Missouri standard is
      not settled for site purposes.
  - ***Ershick v. United Missouri Bank of Kansas City*** (10th Cir. 1991, a Lenexa company).
    - A directed trustee that bought at an independent appraiser's price was not liable for a
      later decline.
    - A trustee that was also the company's secured lender did not commit a per se prohibited
      transaction.
    - It is older, applies deferential review, and involved purchases from employees rather than
      an owner's exit sale.
- **Reconciling with the Polsinelli call.** The call described DOL's central demand as treating an
  ESOP deal no differently from third-party M&A. The primary sources frame it as a *prudent,
  independent, documented investigation*. Doeren Mayhew and Menke add that the process differs in
  form from M&A: the seller makes the first offer and hires the counterparty. The price standard is
  arm's-length.
