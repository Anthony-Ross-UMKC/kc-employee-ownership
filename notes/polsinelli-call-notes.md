# Call with Sean Power, Polsinelli — September 11, 2026

Sean Power, Shareholder, heads Polsinelli's ESOP group — the largest ESOP practice of any law
firm in the country. On the call: Anthony, Camron, Kirby. Recorded and transcribed.

Numbers below are his, and he repeatedly flagged that several were spitballed rather than
looked up. Those are marked. **Nothing here is a citation** — it is a very good lead.

---

## The headline: cost is not the barrier

He took the premise apart directly, and twice.

> "I think the main reason is probably **less cost and more being aware that it's out there**."

> "By the way, they're **no more expensive than a third-party M&A deal and probably less
> expensive** than a third-party M&A deal all in."

> "I don't want to pop the balloon totally, but I'm not totally sure that's exactly the
> biggest problem."

If an ESOP already costs less than the transaction an owner would otherwise do, transaction
cost cannot be what stops them. That kills the version of our thesis that says standardizing
documents unlocks more deals by making them cheaper for sellers.

## What actually stops them — the mechanism he described

1. **Owners avoid the question.** "Most people don't want to think about their own demise…
   there will be a million things every day barking at them to not be doing this kind of
   planning." He has seen companies simply close when the founder died without a plan —
   "more than I care to admit."
2. **They hear about ESOPs socially** — "someone at the country club."
3. **They Google it and get mixed signals** — "depending on whose Reddit thread they read."
4. **They call a trusted advisor, and the incentives break there.**
   - **Investment bankers are paid a success fee**, and ESOP deals go to specialist boutiques.
     "Every time one of those ESOPs comes up, we don't win that deal… We don't get paid. So
     they usually say to them, don't do an ESOP. They're complicated. They're hard. They're
     expensive."
   - **CPAs are hourly and less conflicted**, so they are likelier to raise it. Most of
     Polsinelli's ESOP referrals come from that direction.

This is a distribution and incentive problem with a specific, named failure point.

## The other barrier: carry cost, not transaction cost

For smaller companies the recurring burden matters more than the deal:

- Form 5500 filing, and an audit above a certain employee count
- A third party administrator every year
- Annual independent valuation
- Trustee fee — **roughly $20,000/year**, size-dependent
- "You don't have a solid finance function, you don't have a solid HR function, where it just
  kind of doesn't make sense."

He pointed at NCEO as having worked this problem "for years, maybe decades," and at employee
ownership trusts as the commonly cited answer for companies too small for an ESOP.

---

## Who gets paid, and roughly how much

He walked the full player list. Legal is **"maybe 20%"** of total, and later, adding trustee
counsel, **"probably more than 20%"** — both explicitly spitballed.

**Buy side (the ESOP trust):**

- **Trustee.** Must be independent; an internal trustee on a purchase is an inherent conflict.
  Fees scale with deal size. Ongoing annual fee around $20,000.
- **Independent financial advisor** for the valuation — Stout, Prairie Capital, Chartwell,
  "there's a million of them."
- **Trustee's own counsel.** Trustees outsource this even when they have in-house lawyers.

**Sell side:**

- **Sell-side advisor** — models debt capacity, the warrant package, internal rate of return
  on the seller notes. **About 80% of the industry charges a success fee, typically 1% of
  transaction value.** A minority bill hourly; he named **EFS (Kelly)** as marketing that
  distinction on LinkedIn. "There's a surprising amount of confusion in the marketplace on
  that."
- **Company counsel** — usually does not know ESOPs. Typically calls one of the
  **50 to 100 ESOP lawyers in the country**, many of them sole practitioners or small shops.
- **TPA / record keeper** — tracks allocations and forfeitures at the individual account
  level, as distinct from the trustee's plan-level work.

## Document variability — the most useful thing he gave us

He put numbers on it, all self-described as spitballed:

| Document | Variability | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stock purchase and redemption agreement | **~40%** | The master document; longest; ties everything together |
| Loan and pledge agreement | 10–15% | Sits behind the seller note |
| Internal loan | 10–15% | |
| Seller notes | **~10%** | Fill in maturity, rate; prepayment varies with what the senior lender allows |
| Warrants | "pretty standard" | Differences on callable/putable terms |
| ESOP plan document | already automated | IRS pre-approved documents exist |

On the stock purchase agreement's 40%: "There's going to be a lot of boilerplate, so to speak,
but it also depends on the kind of business you're buying too. The reps and the warranties will
be the same for all businesses, but then there's going to be special reps and warranties for a
software company versus a construction company."

**Three decisions drive nearly all the variation:**

1. **Section 1042 or not** — determines whether you close as a C corp or an S corp, and whether
   the stock must all go to the ESOP rather than to the company. "The first big fork in the
   road."
2. **Seller financed or bank financed** — "makes a lot of differences in the documents."
3. **Partial or full ESOP sale.**

Eight combinations, which matches his estimate that automation "would really need probably
**five or six sets**" of forms.

**On the plan document specifically:** it must be tax-qualified under § 401(a), runs about 100
pages, and "has to say 89 things, I'm making up the number, in order to make it a qualified
plan. And if you say them wrong, it's not a qualified plan anymore." Pre-approved documents
have already automated this. The trap is amendments — **the IRS stopped issuing determination
letters on amendments around 2014**, so "you're kind of just like, I don't know if that's right
or not."

## Financing: one half standardizes, the other never will

> "By financing, what I mean there is the **seller financing piece, not the senior financing
> piece**. The issue with senior financing is the bank… they're going to care very much about
> using their forms. They're never going to use our credit agreement or security agreement or
> anything."

And even on seller notes, "the bank always wants to micromanage what you've got in there to
make sure it's truly subordinated. So they'll change that every time to their exact language."

Polsinelli already works from something close to a standardized set on the financing documents.

## Deal size — what the floor actually is

- Typical fit starts around **$20 million of equity value**.
- He has done a **$5.5 million ESOP with 18 employees** and "it did feel kind of too small."
- Currently pitching one at **$14 million**, "also really small."
- Polsinelli's own deal flow is mostly **$50–60 million and up**.
- Below that there is a "**B team or C team**" of lawyers, sell-side advisors and valuation
  firms who do smaller deals more cheaply. "There is a bit of you get what you pay for" — when
  those companies grow and Polsinelli reviews the documents, "they have a lot of mistakes and
  there's some kind of strategic errors."

He was candid about the bias: 1,200-person firm, 25 offices, "our rates aren't $200 an hour."

## The hard problem he named at the end

Unprompted, and it is the one Absher was already chasing:

> "I think what Professor Absher and I were talking about before is he was looking to solve for
> that **more cash at the closing table** piece… lots of time and money has been put trying to
> crack that piece. And that's just kind of a hard problem to crack because that subordinated
> money is going to be more expensive than the bank's senior lender money."

The shape of it: an ESOP seller rarely gets more than **30% cash at closing**; the remaining
~70% is subordinated seller debt, and the seller "is going to stick around because that seller
note is going to be 7 to 10 years long." Warrants exist to enhance the seller's return on that
note. 100% cash at closing through mezzanine financing is "super, super, super rare."

That, not legal fees, is what a private equity buyer beats.

## Why he wants automation anyway

This matters — the demand comes from the supply side, not from sellers:

> "We as a group, we're wanting to get more efficient… **we have more work than we can do**. And
> so any place we can get quicker or more automated is a lovely innovation for us. And frankly,
> we're not going to just expand our margins on less time — **that'll get passed through**."

They have already tried building a master purchase-side document and found it "a really
strenuous exercise." He also noted forms circulate freely in a small community — "you'll see
your own forms just floating around in different parts of the ecosystem" — with regional
pockets where the same documents recur.

## Due diligence

- The checklist he offered is the **trustee team's legal DDRL**, and "will look almost
  identical to a third party M&A due diligence list."
- **DOL's central demand is that an ESOP deal be no different from a third-party M&A deal.**
  "If you go too fast, or if you shortcut on due diligence, then the regulators will come and
  say you did a shoddy deal, it was a fixed fight."
- The financial diligence is separate and "just as important" — the trustee's independent
  financial advisor runs its own request list, "a poor man's quality of earnings study." We are
  only getting the legal half.
- Even good ESOP diligence "is still lighter" than private equity diligence, eight or nine
  times out of ten. There is already cost pressure on the space.
- **Rep and warranty insurance ESOP deals** exist and he finds them interesting — the RWI
  underwriter relies on the buyer's diligence and hires its own lawyers to test it, which
  raises the diligence burden meaningfully.

## Trustees

More varied than it looks. Institutional versus individual; a handful of corporate trust
departments; many small regional shops. "I'm dismayed every day when someone in our group will
send an email… hey, has anyone ever heard of this trustee?" Some smaller trustees keep costs
down with practices that are "not that good."

## Questions this raises for the team

These are for the group to decide, not settled. Listed because the call bears on each one.

- **Is transaction cost still the problem we are working on?** Sean says an ESOP already costs
  less than the third-party sale an owner would otherwise do. If that is right, what does
  lowering cost accomplish?
- **If standardization is worth doing, who is it for?** He described his own group as
  capacity-constrained and said efficiency gains get passed through. That is a different buyer
  than the seller we had assumed, and it may or may not be the one we want to serve.
- **How much of the document set is actually worth attacking?** His numbers put seller notes
  around 10% variable and the stock purchase agreement around 40%, with the plan document
  already automated and senior debt documents unavailable. Whether that residue is worth a
  semester is a judgment call.
- **Is awareness the more promising target?** It is the barrier he believes in, and he
  described a specific failure point in the advisor chain. It is also closer to what we
  originally sketched, for entirely different reasons.
- **Does the cash-at-closing gap belong in scope at all?** It is the real economic constraint
  he named, Absher has already been working on it, and Sean says serious money has failed at
  it. That cuts both ways.
